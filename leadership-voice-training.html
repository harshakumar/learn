A WORKING MANUAL

The Leadership Voice

Leadership language isn't about sounding important. It's about removing the friction between what you mean and what people hear — so your team can act on it. This manual is built around real phrase swaps, not theory.

01

Four foundations

Before the specific phrases, these are the habits everything else is built on.

Say the thing first

Lead with the point, then the reasoning. Most people do the reverse — they build up context and land the conclusion last. That reads as uncertain, even when the thinking is sound. State the decision or ask, then explain.

Cut the hedge, keep the humility

"I think maybe we should possibly consider" isn't humble — it's unclear. Real humility sounds like "here's my read, tell me what I'm missing," not a sentence full of soft exits.

Let silence do work

A pause after a hard statement isn't a gap to fill — it's the moment the room absorbs what you said. Rushing to soften or explain after a clear statement undercuts it.

Own outcomes in first person

"We're behind because the team didn't align" distances you from the result. "I didn't get us aligned in time" is harder to say and far more credible — and it's usually just as true.

02

Framing: statements over questions

Asking "what do you all think?" before you've said anything hands the room your job. Offer a position, then invite challenge to it.

DIFFUSE

"What does everyone think we should do about the launch date?"

DIRECT

"I want to push the launch two weeks. Here's why — and I want to hear where this breaks."

DIFFUSE

"I was just wondering if maybe we could look at the budget again?"

DIRECT

"The budget needs revisiting before Friday. Can we get 30 minutes today?"

DIFFUSE

"This might be a bad idea, but what if we tried a different vendor?"

DIRECT

"I think we should switch vendors. Here's the trade-off I'm weighing."

03

Feedback that lands

Vague feedback protects your comfort, not theirs. Use a fixed structure: what happened, what it affected, what you want next. Skip the sandwich — it trains people to distrust your praise.

Correcting a missed deadline

VAGUE

"Just a heads up, the report was a little late — no big deal, just try to keep an eye on timing going forward!"

STRUCTURED

"The report came in two days late, which pushed the client call back. I need it by end of day Thursday going forward — what got in the way this time?"

The structured version names the fact, the impact, and the change — then opens the floor. It's not harsher, it's clearer, which is what actually respects the person.

Giving credit

VAGUE

"Great job on the deck, everyone!"

STRUCTURED

"Priya's framing in slide 4 is why the client stopped pushing back on price. That's the moment that changed the room."

Specific praise is remembered and repeated. Generic praise is forgotten by lunch.

Drill: the three-part structure +

Situation — name what happened, concretely, without adjectives ("late" not "sloppy").
Impact — what it affected, in real terms (a client, a deadline, a teammate's workload).
Forward — the specific change you want, or the question that gets you there.

Practice: write one piece of feedback you've been avoiding, using only these three parts. No qualifiers, no "but overall you're doing great" unless it's true and separate from this point.

04

Announcing decisions

A decision announcement has a shape: the call, the reasoning, what happens next, and where input still matters. Missing any piece reads as either arbitrary or wishy-washy.

Reversing a prior decision

EVASIVE

"So, um, some things have changed and we're kind of going a different way now, sorry for the confusion."

OWNED

"I'm changing course from what I said last week. The new data on churn changed my read. Here's the new plan, and here's what stays the same."

Naming the reversal directly costs less credibility than people fear. Burying it costs more — people notice the shift either way.

Closing a debate to move forward

EVASIVE

"I guess we've talked about this a lot, so maybe let's just go with option B for now?"

OWNED

"We've heard both sides. We're going with option B. I know that's not everyone's first choice — here's the reasoning, and here's how we'll know if it's wrong."

Drill: the four-part decision +

The call — stated in one sentence, no hedge.
The reasoning — the one or two factors that actually drove it, not a full audit trail.
What happens next — concrete, dated if possible.
Where input still counts — be honest about what's fixed and what's still open. Fake openness erodes trust fast.

05

Under pressure

Pressure is where filler words and hedges multiply. The fix isn't performing calm — it's having a shorter sentence ready.

UNDER PRESSURE

"Um, so, I mean, there's a lot going on, and honestly I'm not 100% sure yet, but we're kind of looking into it, I think?"

GROUNDED

"I don't have the full picture yet. Here's what I know, here's what I'm finding out, and I'll update you by 4pm."

UNDER PRESSURE

"I'm so sorry, I really should have caught this, I feel terrible, this is totally on me, I'm so sorry again—"

GROUNDED

"I missed this. Here's the fix, and here's what I'm changing so it doesn't happen again."

One clean acknowledgment beats three anxious ones. Repeated apologizing shifts the room's attention to managing your discomfort instead of solving the problem.

07

Before your next meeting

A short pre-check. Click each item as you build the habit — this list resets when you close the page, so use it as a rehearsal, not a record.

I know the one sentence I need to land, and I can say it without a lead-in.
I've cut "just," "maybe," and "I think" from my opening line.
If I'm giving feedback, I have the situation, impact, and forward step ready.
If I'm announcing a decision, I know what's fixed and what's still open for input.
I've planned where I'll pause instead of filling silence.
If something went wrong, I've written one clean sentence owning it — not three anxious ones.

None of this is about volume or authority theater. The through-line is clarity: say the real thing, in the fewest words that still respect the listener's intelligence. The habit builds with repetition, not with a single good meeting.
