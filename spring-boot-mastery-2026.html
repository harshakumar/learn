1.1 The State of Java and Spring Boot in the UK, 2026

If you open ten senior backend job specifications on any given week from a UK finance, insurance, government, or retail employer, eight or nine of them will mention Spring Boot. This has been true for the better part of a decade, and in 2026 it remains true — not because the ecosystem has stood still, but because it hasn't. Spring Boot 4, built on Spring Framework 7, has absorbed the last five years of JVM evolution — Virtual Threads, structured concurrency, ahead-of-time processing, and first-class support for native compilation — without asking enterprises to abandon the programming model that made Spring dominant in the first place.

The UK market has its own character. Unlike Silicon Valley-style startups that will happily rewrite a service in a new framework every eighteen months, UK enterprises — particularly regulated ones — operate under constraints that reward stability, auditability and a deep talent pool. Spring Boot wins on all three. There are more Spring-literate developers in London, Manchester, Leeds, Bristol and Edinburgh than for any competing JVM framework combined, which materially reduces hiring risk for CTOs and lowers the bus-factor for engineering leads.

1.2 Sector Deep Dive: Finance, Insurance, Government, Retail, Consulting

Spring Boot's dominance is not evenly distributed across industries — but it is broad. Understanding the sector you're targeting changes what you should emphasise in interviews and in your day-to-day engineering choices.

Financial Services

Tier-1 and challenger banks, payment processors, and asset managers are the single largest employer segment for Spring Boot developers in the UK. Expect heavy emphasis on transactional integrity, auditability, resilience patterns (circuit breakers, retries, idempotency), and strict change-control processes. Kafka-based event architectures and strong observability requirements (driven by operational resilience regulation) are near-universal. Native image compilation is gaining traction here specifically to reduce cold-start costs in serverless payment-processing paths.

Insurance

Insurers (Lloyd's syndicates, general insurers, insurtechs) lean on Spring Boot for policy administration, claims platforms, and increasingly for AI-assisted underwriting and claims triage — which is why Spring AI 2.0 gets a full chapter in this book. Data retention and GDPR compliance are first-class engineering concerns, not afterthoughts, given the sensitivity of claims data.

Government & Public Sector

Departments aligned with Government Digital Service (GDS) standards favour Spring Boot for its maturity, its extensive security tooling, and the depth of the supplier ecosystem able to support it under public-sector procurement rules. Accessibility, auditability, and open standards (OpenAPI, GOV.UK Design System-adjacent API conventions) matter more here than almost anywhere else.

Retail & E-commerce

Retailers care about one thing above all in November and December: surviving peak load. Spring Boot's maturity around resilience, caching, and horizontal scaling on Kubernetes makes it a safe choice for checkout and inventory services that must not fall over on Black Friday. Reactive WebFlux sees genuine production use here, more than in most other sectors.

Consulting & Systems Integrators

Big consultancies (and their delivery arms) staff Spring Boot engineers across all of the above sectors on client engagements. Consulting roles reward breadth — comfort moving between reactive and imperative styles, between cloud providers, and between greenfield builds and legacy modernisation — more than deep specialisation in any one domain.

1.4 What "Enterprise-Grade" Means in a UK Regulated Context

This book's subtitle promises "enterprise-grade" applications, and that phrase carries specific weight in the UK. It is not a synonym for "large" or "complex." An enterprise-grade Spring Boot application, in the sense used throughout this book, satisfies all of the following:

  • Auditable: every state-changing action can be traced to an actor, a time, and a reason, satisfying internal audit and external regulatory review.
  • Secure by default: authentication and authorisation are enforced at the framework level, not bolted on per-endpoint; secrets never live in source control or plaintext configuration.
  • GDPR-aware by design: personal data is identified, minimised, and given an explicit retention and deletion policy from the first schema migration, not retrofitted after a Data Protection Impact Assessment flags it.
  • Observable: metrics, structured logs, and distributed traces exist before the first production incident, not after it.
  • Resilient: the application degrades gracefully under partial failure of its dependencies rather than cascading that failure outward.
  • Testable and tested: a real test pyramid exists, including integration tests against real infrastructure via Testcontainers, not just mocked unit tests.
  • Deployable without heroics: a new engineer can ship a change to production via a documented, automated pipeline on their first week.

Every chapter from Chapter 4 onward builds toward these properties concretely, with runnable code. Chapter 1 is context; Chapter 2 onward is where you start typing.

1.5 Spring Boot 4 and Spring Framework 7: What Actually Changed

Spring Boot 4, sitting on Spring Framework 7, is best understood as a consolidation release rather than a reinvention. If you're coming from Spring Boot 3.x, most of your muscle memory transfers directly. The headline shifts that matter for enterprise teams are:

  • Java 17 as the floor, Java 21/25 as the recommended baseline. Virtual Threads and structured concurrency are treated as first-class runtime concerns, not experimental opt-ins.
  • Deeper AOT (Ahead-of-Time) processing integrated into the default build, making GraalVM native-image builds a routine CI step rather than a specialist exercise.
  • RFC 9457 Problem Details as the default shape for error responses across Spring MVC and WebFlux, standardising an area that previously varied team-to-team.
  • Refined observability defaults via Micrometer and OpenTelemetry, with less manual wiring required to get traces flowing end-to-end.
  • Spring AI 2.0 as a mainstream, first-party dependency rather than an incubating side project — reflecting how quickly AI features moved from "innovation team pilot" to "line-of-business requirement" across UK finance and insurance.
  • Jakarta EE 11 alignment, continuing the namespace and specification modernisation that began with the javax → jakarta migration in Spring Boot 3.

Why this matters commercially Before Virtual Threads, the honest answer to "how do we get reactive-style throughput without rewriting our imperative codebase" was "you can't, not cheaply." UK teams sitting on large Spring MVC estates — which is most of them — can now absorb a meaningful concurrency upgrade by changing a configuration flag and re-testing, rather than commissioning a multi-quarter WebFlux rewrite. This is one of the single biggest reasons Spring Boot 4 adoption has moved quickly through UK enterprises in 2026: it de-risks a decision that used to force a framework-versus-framework debate.

1.6 Java 21/25 as the New Enterprise Baseline

For years, UK enterprises — particularly banks running risk-averse change processes — treated Java 8 (and later Java 11) as a comfortable, indefinitely-supportable baseline. That comfort has eroded. Spring Boot 4's minimum supported version, the security and performance benefits of Virtual Threads, and vendor support timelines have combined to make Java 21 the practical floor for new work and Java 25 (the current LTS at time of writing) the recommended target for genuinely new platforms.

OrderEvent.java — sealed interfaces and record patterns in domain code
public sealed interface OrderEvent
        permits OrderPlaced, OrderCancelled, OrderRefunded {}

public record OrderPlaced(String orderId, BigDecimal amount, Instant placedAt) implements OrderEvent {}
public record OrderCancelled(String orderId, String reason, Instant cancelledAt) implements OrderEvent {}
public record OrderRefunded(String orderId, BigDecimal amount, Instant refundedAt) implements OrderEvent {}

public String describe(OrderEvent event) {
    return switch (event) {
        case OrderPlaced(var id, var amount, var at) ->
            "Order %s placed for £%s at %s".formatted(id, amount, at);
        case OrderCancelled(var id, var reason, var at) ->
            "Order %s cancelled (%s) at %s".formatted(id, reason, at);
        case OrderRefunded(var id, var amount, var at) ->
            "Order %s refunded £%s at %s".formatted(id, amount, at);
    };
}

Notice that this switch expression is exhaustive: the compiler enforces that every permitted subtype of OrderEvent is handled. In a financial-services audit trail, that compiler-enforced exhaustiveness is not a style preference — it is a defect-prevention mechanism that used to require a checklist and a code reviewer's memory.

2.1 Installing JDK 25 (LTS) and Toolchains via SDKMAN

We touched on toolchain installation in Chapter 1; here we go deeper, because getting this wrong is the most common source of "it works locally but not in CI" friction during a Spring Boot 4 migration. SDKMAN remains the pragmatic default for UK teams managing multiple JDK versions across legacy and modernised services on the same laptop.

# List available JDK 25 distributions
sdk list java | grep 25

# Install Eclipse Temurin's build (recommended default for enterprise use)
sdk install java 25-tem

# Confirm and pin as default
sdk default java 25-tem
java -version

If your organisation standardises on a specific vendor build (Amazon Corretto, Azul Zulu, or Oracle's own distribution) for support-contract reasons, install that instead — the JDK specification is identical, but your procurement and support agreements are not.

Gradle and Maven Toolchains

Enterprise teams frequently run a build server on one JDK while developers run several locally. Both Maven and Gradle support a toolchain declaration that decouples "which JDK builds this project" from "which JDK launched the build tool," which avoids an entire category of environment-drift bugs.

pom.xml — toolchain enforcement
<build>
  <plugins>
    <plugin>
      <groupId>org.apache.maven.plugins</groupId>
      <artifactId>maven-toolchains-plugin</artifactId>
      <version>3.2.0</version>
      <executions>
        <execution>
          <goals><goal>toolchain</goal></goals>
        </execution>
      </executions>
      <configuration>
        <toolchains>
          <jdk>
            <version>25</version>
            <vendor>temurin</vendor>
          </jdk>
        </toolchains>
      </configuration>
    </plugin>
  </plugins>
</build>

2.5 Virtual Threads: Enabling and Understanding the Runtime Model

We enabled Virtual Threads with a single property in Chapter 1. Here's what actually happens underneath, because interviewers at senior level will expect you to explain it, not just enable it.

Before Java 21, every blocking request handled by Spring MVC (a JDBC call, a synchronous RestTemplate call to a downstream service) tied up one OS-level platform thread for the duration of that call. Tomcat's default thread pool (typically 200 threads) becomes your effective concurrency ceiling — the two-hundred-and-first concurrent slow downstream call queues, even though your CPU is sitting idle waiting on I/O.

Virtual Threads change the unit of concurrency. Each request is handled on a cheap, JVM-managed virtual thread. When that virtual thread blocks on I/O, it's unmounted from its underlying platform (carrier) thread, which is freed to run other virtual threads. Your existing blocking, imperative code — the code most UK Spring MVC estates are built from — gets reactive-style throughput without a single line of business logic changing.

application.yml
spring:
  threads:
    virtual:
      enabled: true
ClaimsController.java — unchanged imperative code, now running on virtual threads
@RestController
@RequestMapping("/api/v1/claims")
public class ClaimsController {

    private final ClaimsService claimsService;

    public ClaimsController(ClaimsService claimsService) {
        this.claimsService = claimsService;
    }

    @GetMapping("/{claimId}")
    public ClaimResponse getClaim(@PathVariable String claimId) {
        // This call blocks — a JDBC round trip — exactly as before.
        // Under Virtual Threads, that block no longer pins a platform thread.
        return claimsService.findClaim(claimId);
    }
}
Common Pitfall Virtual Threads do not help — and can actively hurt — code with long synchronized blocks around blocking I/O, because prior to full support maturity a virtual thread blocked inside a synchronized block pins its carrier thread instead of unmounting. Audit legacy code for synchronized guarding database or network calls before flipping this switch on a large estate; prefer java.util.concurrent.locks.ReentrantLock in new code for exactly this reason.

2.6 Structured Concurrency in Service Code

Structured concurrency gives you a disciplined way to fan out several concurrent calls and fan them back in as a single unit — with cancellation and error propagation handled correctly by default, instead of manually managed Future plumbing. This is exactly the shape of a common enterprise problem: aggregating data from several downstream services to build one API response.

ClaimSummaryService.java
package uk.co.acmebank.claims.service;

import org.springframework.stereotype.Service;

import java.util.concurrent.StructuredTaskScope;

@Service
public class ClaimSummaryService {

    private final PolicyClient policyClient;
    private final CustomerClient customerClient;
    private final ClaimHistoryClient claimHistoryClient;

    public ClaimSummaryService(PolicyClient policyClient,
                                CustomerClient customerClient,
                                ClaimHistoryClient claimHistoryClient) {
        this.policyClient = policyClient;
        this.customerClient = customerClient;
        this.claimHistoryClient = claimHistoryClient;
    }

    public ClaimSummary buildSummary(String claimId, String policyId, String customerId) throws Exception {
        try (var scope = new StructuredTaskScope.ShutdownOnFailure()) {

            var policy = scope.fork(() -> policyClient.fetch(policyId));
            var customer = scope.fork(() -> customerClient.fetch(customerId));
            var history = scope.fork(() -> claimHistoryClient.fetchHistory(claimId));

            scope.join();           // wait for all forks
            scope.throwIfFailed();  // propagate first failure, cancelling the rest

            return new ClaimSummary(policy.get(), customer.get(), history.get());
        }
    }
}

Three downstream calls run concurrently on virtual threads. If any one of them fails, ShutdownOnFailure cancels the others automatically rather than leaving them running to completion and wasting downstream capacity — a subtle but real cost saving at scale, and a correctness improvement over hand-rolled CompletableFuture.allOf chains, which don't cancel siblings on failure without extra code.

UK Job Market Tip "Structured concurrency" is a phrase that has started appearing verbatim in senior/lead Spring Boot job specs at UK banks in 2026, specifically because it addresses a well-known pain point (silent resource leaks from unmanaged Future fan-out) that many interviewers have personally been burned by. Have a concrete example ready — like the aggregation service above — rather than a textbook definition.

3.1 The IoC Container and ApplicationContext Lifecycle Revisited

Every Spring Boot developer can define a @Bean. Fewer can explain, under interview pressure, exactly what happens when SpringApplication.run() executes — and that gap is precisely what separates "senior" from "mid-level" in a UK technical interview loop. It's worth re-grounding this before we get to auto-configuration, because auto-configuration is just a very large, very conditional set of @Bean definitions competing for a place in the same container.

At a high level, SpringApplication.run():

  1. Determines the application type (servlet, reactive, or none) from the classpath.
  2. Creates the appropriate ApplicationContext implementation (AnnotationConfigServletWebServerApplicationContext for a typical Spring MVC app).
  3. Loads and applies ApplicationContextInitializers and fires early ApplicationListener events (ApplicationStartingEvent, then ApplicationEnvironmentPreparedEvent).
  4. Prepares the Environment — merging command-line args, environment variables, profile-specific YAML, and defaults, in a well-defined precedence order.
  5. Registers your @SpringBootApplication-annotated class as a bean definition source, then triggers component scanning and auto-configuration processing.
  6. Refreshes the context: bean definitions are resolved, dependency graphs built, beans instantiated in dependency order, BeanPostProcessors applied (this is where AOP proxies, @Transactional wrapping, and validation happen), and lifecycle callbacks (@PostConstruct, SmartLifecycle) invoked.
  7. Starts the embedded web server (Tomcat, by default) and begins serving requests.
  8. Fires ApplicationReadyEvent — the point most teams wire their readiness probes to.
UK Job Market Tip A frequent senior-interview question at UK banks: "what's the difference between ApplicationReadyEvent and ContextRefreshedEvent, and which would you use for a Kubernetes readiness probe?" The honest answer is that ApplicationReadyEvent fires after the web server has started and the application is genuinely ready to serve traffic, whereas ContextRefreshedEvent fires earlier and doesn't guarantee the server is listening — a distinction that matters directly for avoiding failed requests during a rolling deployment (Chapter 10).

3.2 Auto-Configuration Internals: Conditions, Ordering, Reports

Auto-configuration classes are ordinary @Configuration classes, registered via a file at META-INF/spring/org.springframework.boot.autoconfigure.AutoConfiguration.imports inside each starter JAR (this replaced the older spring.factories mechanism back in Boot 2.7, and remains the mechanism in Boot 4). What makes them "auto" is that almost every bean definition inside is guarded by a @Conditional annotation.

AnnotationBean is registered when…
@ConditionalOnClassa given class is present on the classpath
@ConditionalOnMissingBeanthe developer hasn't already defined their own bean of that type
@ConditionalOnPropertya configuration property matches an expected value (or is simply present)
@ConditionalOnWebApplicationthe application context is a servlet or reactive web context
@ConditionalOnBeananother specific bean already exists in the context

This is why adding spring-boot-starter-data-jpa to your POM is enough to get a working DataSource, EntityManagerFactory, and transaction manager with zero explicit configuration: Hibernate and the JDBC driver being on the classpath satisfies @ConditionalOnClass, and the absence of your own DataSource bean satisfies @ConditionalOnMissingBean — so Spring Boot's own sensible default steps in.

Reading the Auto-Configuration Report

When auto-configuration doesn't do what you expect, this is your single most useful diagnostic tool — far more useful than breakpoint-stepping through framework internals:

java -jar claims-service.jar --debug

This prints a full report at startup listing every auto-configuration class considered, split into "Positive matches" (conditions satisfied, bean registered) and "Negative matches" (conditions not satisfied, with the specific reason). When a bean you expected is missing, the negative-match section tells you exactly which condition failed — almost always faster than reading source code.

3.9 Case Study: Taming a 400-Bean Legacy Context

A recurring UK consulting engagement pattern: a decade-old Spring application, migrated through Spring Boot 1 through 3 without ever being re-architected, now boots 400+ beans, takes 45 seconds to start locally, and no single engineer fully understands why several beans exist. The practical remediation sequence used across several such UK engagements:

  1. Baseline with the debug report. Capture the full positive/negative match report before touching anything, so you have a diff baseline.
  2. Identify duplicate/competing configuration. Search for repeated @ConditionalOnMissingBean overrides across internal libraries — often a symptom of three different teams each having "fixed" the same problem independently over the years.
  3. Strip unused starters. It's common to find starters (and their entire auto-configuration trees) pulled in transitively years ago for a feature that was since removed. Cross-reference actual bean usage against dependency tree.
  4. Introduce explicit component scanning boundaries. An unbounded @ComponentScan across an entire monorepo package tree is a common, easily fixed source of unnecessary bean registration — scope it to the packages the service actually owns.
  5. Move to lazy initialization selectively (spring.main.lazy-initialization=true) as a diagnostic, not a permanent fix — it surfaces which beans are genuinely needed at startup versus only on first use, informing further cleanup, but can mask real startup-time failures if left enabled in production.

    }

IdempotencyService.java
@Service
public class IdempotencyService {

    private final IdempotencyKeyRepository repository;

    public IdempotencyService(IdempotencyKeyRepository repository) {
        this.repository = repository;
    }

    @Transactional
    public <T> ResponseEntity<T> executeOnce(String key, Supplier<ResponseEntity<T>> action) {
        return repository.findCachedResponse(key, action.getClass())
                .map(cached -> (ResponseEntity<T>) cached)
                .orElseGet(() -> {
                    ResponseEntity<T> result = action.get();
                    repository.store(key, result);
                    return result;
                });
    }
}

5.2 Entity Design with Records and Immutability Trade-Offs

JPA entities cannot be Java records — JPA requires a no-argument constructor and mutable fields for its proxying and lazy-loading machinery, which is fundamentally incompatible with record immutability. This is a common point of confusion for developers who've absorbed "prefer records" as a blanket rule from earlier chapters. The correct pattern: entities stay as traditional mutable classes; everything that crosses a boundary (API DTOs, as in Chapter 4; read-model projections, below) is a record.

Claim.java
@Entity
@Table(name = "claims")
public class Claim {

    @Id
    private String id;

    @Column(nullable = false)
    private String policyId;

    @Column(nullable = false)
    private BigDecimal amount;

    @Enumerated(EnumType.STRING)
    private ClaimStatusEnum status;

    private Instant createdAt;

    @Version
    private Long version; // optimistic locking

    protected Claim() {} // required by JPA

    public Claim(String id, String policyId, BigDecimal amount) {
        this.id = id;
        this.policyId = policyId;
        this.amount = amount;
        this.status = ClaimStatusEnum.SUBMITTED;
        this.createdAt = Instant.now();
    }

    // getters, and only the specific mutating methods the domain actually needs —
    // avoid blanket setters that let any caller mutate any field from anywhere.
    public void approve(BigDecimal settlementAmount) {
        if (this.status != ClaimStatusEnum.UNDER_REVIEW) {
            throw new IllegalStateException("Cannot approve a claim not under review");
        }
        this.status = ClaimStatusEnum.APPROVED;
        this.amount = settlementAmount;
    }
}

Note @Version for optimistic locking, and the deliberate absence of a blanket setStatus() — state transitions go through named domain methods (approve()) that enforce invariants, rather than allowing any caller to set an entity into an invalid state.

5.4 Projections and DTO-First Reads for Performance

Chapter 4 flagged N+1 queries surfacing through paginated API responses as a common production issue. Interface-based and record-based projections solve this at the source by fetching exactly the columns needed, in a single query, rather than hydrating full entity graphs.

ClaimSummaryProjection.java — record-based DTO projection
public interface ClaimRepository extends JpaRepository<Claim, String> {

    @Query("""
        select new uk.co.acmebank.claims.repository.ClaimListItem(
            c.id, c.policyId, c.amount, c.status)
        from Claim c
        where c.status = :status
        """)
    Page<ClaimListItem> findSummariesByStatus(@Param("status") ClaimStatusEnum status, Pageable pageable);
}

public record ClaimListItem(String id, String policyId, BigDecimal amount, ClaimStatusEnum status) {}

This single query returns exactly four columns per row, with no lazy-loaded association ever touched — eliminating the entire class of N+1 bug for this endpoint by construction, not by discipline.

5.5 Transactions: Propagation, Isolation, and Pitfalls Under Virtual Threads

Spring's @Transactional semantics are unchanged by Virtual Threads — a transaction is still bound to the calling thread via a ThreadLocal-based resource holder, and virtual threads carry their own thread-local state correctly. The pitfall is not correctness, but a subtler resource-exhaustion risk: a long-running transaction that blocks on a slow downstream call while holding a database connection now scales to far higher concurrency under Virtual Threads than it would have under a bounded platform-thread pool — which means it can exhaust your connection pool far faster than the old model ever allowed.

Common Pitfall Under Virtual Threads, a service that previously self-limited to ~200 concurrent in-flight requests (Tomcat's default platform thread pool) can now accept thousands concurrently. If even a fraction of those requests hold a database connection inside a @Transactional method that also makes a slow external call, your connection pool — typically sized far below 1000 — becomes the new bottleneck, and starts failing fast rather than queueing gracefully. Keep transactions short and never make a slow, non-database network call from inside a @Transactional method; fetch external data first, then open the transaction only for the database work.

ClaimApprovalService.java — correct ordering
public void approveClaim(String claimId) {
    // 1. Slow external call — happens OUTSIDE any open transaction / DB connection.
    FraudCheckResult fraudCheck = fraudCheckClient.check(claimId);

    // 2. Short, focused transaction — connection held only for the DB work.
    applyApproval(claimId, fraudCheck);
}

@Transactional
protected void applyApproval(String claimId, FraudCheckResult fraudCheck) {
    Claim claim = claimRepository.findById(claimId).orElseThrow();
    claim.approve(fraudCheck.approvedAmount());
}

6.5 GDPR by Design: Data Minimisation, Consent, Subject Access Requests

UK GDPR (retained EU law, enforced by the ICO post-Brexit) is not primarily a legal-team concern that engineers implement after the fact — the strongest UK engineering teams treat it as a design constraint from the first schema, in the same category as security.

  • Data minimisation — every column added to a table should be traceable to a specific business need; "we might want this later" is not sufficient justification for storing personal data.
  • Purpose limitation in code, not just policy — a service that fetches customer data for fraud checking should not incidentally expose that same data through an unrelated endpoint just because the entity happens to be loaded.
  • Subject Access Request (SAR) support — build a genuine, tested capability to export all personal data held about a given individual across every service that touches it, not a one-off script written under deadline pressure when the first SAR arrives.

@Service
public class SubjectAccessRequestService {

6.6 Audit Logging and Tamper-Evidence for FCA/PRA Environments

Financial-services regulators (the FCA and PRA in the UK) expect audit trails that are not just present, but demonstrably tamper-evident — an auditor needs confidence that a log entry showing an approval hasn't been quietly edited after the fact.

AuditLogEntry.java — hash-chained audit entries
@Entity
@Table(name = "audit_log")
public class AuditLogEntry {

    @Id
    private UUID id;
    private String actor;
    private String action;
    private String subjectId;
    private Instant occurredAt;
    private String payloadHash;
    private String previousEntryHash; // links this entry to the one before it

    public static AuditLogEntry create(String actor, String action, String subjectId,
                                        String payload, String previousHash) {
        var entry = new AuditLogEntry();
        entry.id = UUID.randomUUID();
        entry.actor = actor;
        entry.action = action;
        entry.subjectId = subjectId;
        entry.occurredAt = Instant.now();
        entry.previousEntryHash = previousHash;
        entry.payloadHash = sha256(payload + previousHash);
        return entry;
    }
}

Each entry's hash incorporates the previous entry's hash, forming a chain: altering any historical entry breaks every subsequent hash, making tampering detectable by simple verification rather than requiring forensic analysis. This pattern — a lightweight, application-level hash chain, not a blockchain — is a pragmatic, well-understood technique that satisfies most UK financial-services audit requirements without the operational overhead of distributed-ledger infrastructure.

7.2 Project Reactor Fundamentals: Mono, Flux, Backpressure

Mono<T> represents zero-or-one asynchronous result; Flux<T> represents zero-to-many. Nothing happens until a subscriber subscribes — reactive pipelines are lazy and declarative by design.

ClaimReactiveService.java
@Service
public class ClaimReactiveService {

    private final ClaimReactiveRepository repository;

    public ClaimReactiveService(ClaimReactiveRepository repository) {
        this.repository = repository;
    }

    public Mono<ClaimResponse> findById(String id) {
        return repository.findById(id)
                .map(ClaimResponse::from)
                .switchIfEmpty(Mono.error(new ClaimNotFoundException(id)));
    }

    public Flux<ClaimResponse> findByStatus(ClaimStatusEnum status) {
        return repository.findByStatus(status)
                .map(ClaimResponse::from)
                .onBackpressureBuffer(1000); // bound the buffer explicitly for a fast producer / slow consumer
    }
}

Backpressure is Reactor's actual differentiator over a plain CompletableFuture-based async model: a slow HTTP client consuming a Flux of thousands of claim records signals its demand upstream, and Reactor's operators respect that signal rather than flooding the consumer — this is precisely the property that lets a streaming export endpoint (7.5) serve a huge result set without loading it all into memory.

7.4 WebClient Patterns and Resilience

PolicyReactiveClient.java
@Component
public class PolicyReactiveClient {

    private final WebClient webClient;

    public PolicyReactiveClient(WebClient.Builder builder) {
        this.webClient = builder
                .baseUrl("https://policy-service.internal.acmebank.co.uk")
                .build();
    }

    public Mono<PolicyDetails> fetch(String policyId) {
        return webClient.get()
                .uri("/api/v1/policies/{id}", policyId)
                .retrieve()
                .onStatus(HttpStatusCode::is4xxClientError,
                        response -> Mono.error(new PolicyNotFoundException(policyId)))
                .bodyToMono(PolicyDetails.class)
                .timeout(Duration.ofSeconds(3))
                .retryWhen(Retry.backoff(3, Duration.ofMillis(200))
                        .filter(ex -> ex instanceof WebClientRequestException))
                .transformDeferred(RateLimiterOperator.of(policyRateLimiter))
                .transformDeferred(CircuitBreakerOperator.of(policyCircuitBreaker));
    }
}

7.5 Server-Sent Events and Streaming APIs

This is the scenario where reactive genuinely earns its complexity over Virtual Threads: a live feed of claim-status updates pushed to a dashboard.

ClaimEventsController.java
@RestController
@RequestMapping("/api/v1/claims")
public class ClaimEventsController {

    private final ClaimEventPublisher eventPublisher;

    public ClaimEventsController(ClaimEventPublisher eventPublisher) {
        this.eventPublisher = eventPublisher;
    }

    @GetMapping(value = "/events", produces = MediaType.TEXT_EVENT_STREAM_VALUE)
    public Flux<ServerSentEvent<ClaimResponse>> streamClaimEvents() {
        return eventPublisher.stream()
                .map(claim -> ServerSentEvent.builder(ClaimResponse.from(claim))
                        .id(claim.getId())
                        .event("claim-updated")
                        .build())
                .doOnCancel(() -> log.info("Client disconnected from claim event stream"));
    }
}

Backpressure here isn't academic: without it, a dashboard client on a slow mobile connection falling behind a high-throughput claims system would either buffer unboundedly (a memory leak waiting to happen) or drop the connection entirely. 7.7 When WebFlux Is the Wrong Choice: An Honest Assessment

This section exists because too much material in this space is written by reactive enthusiasts unwilling to name reactive's real costs. In a UK enterprise context specifically:

  • Debugging cost is real and ongoing, not just a one-off learning curve — a stack trace from a failed reactive chain three flatMaps deep is measurably harder to diagnose under production incident pressure than an imperative stack trace, and this matters directly for your Mean Time To Resolution during an incident.
  • Hiring and bench-strength risk. A UK team of ten Spring MVC engineers can safely lose any one of them without a knowledge cliff. A team with one reactive specialist carrying the WebFlux service is a genuine single-point-of-failure risk that a CTO should weigh against the throughput benefit, especially now that Virtual Threads closes most of the gap.
  • Blocking calls inside a reactive chain are a landmine, not a minor bug. A single blocking JDBC call accidentally left inside a Mono.map() can starve Reactor's small, fixed event-loop thread pool far more severely than the equivalent mistake would ever affect a Virtual-Thread-backed MVC service — the failure mode is worse, not just equally bad.
  • Not every driver has a reactive equivalent. Plenty of enterprise systems of record (mainframe integrations, certain legacy SOAP services, some proprietary databases) simply have no R2DBC or reactive client, forcing an ugly blocking-call-wrapped-in-Schedulers.boundedElastic() compromise that undermines the entire point of going reactive for that call path.

The pragmatic, defensible position for most UK enterprise teams in 2026: default to Spring MVC with Virtual Threads for the majority of services, and reach for WebFlux deliberately, service-by-service, only where genuine streaming or backpressure requirements justify its ongoing cost. This is also, not coincidentally, the position most likely to earn respect rather than raised eyebrows in a senior UK technical interview — showing you can make the boring, correct choice rather than the exciting one.

PolicyClient.java — imperative style, decorated declaratively
@Component
public class PolicyClient {

    private final RestClient restClient;

    public PolicyClient(RestClient.Builder builder) {
        this.restClient = builder.baseUrl("https://policy-service.internal.acmebank.co.uk").build();
    }

    @CircuitBreaker(name = "policyService", fallbackMethod = "fallbackPolicy")
    @Retry(name = "policyService")
    @Bulkhead(name = "policyService")
    public PolicyDetails fetch(String policyId) {
        return restClient.get()
                .uri("/api/v1/policies/{id}", policyId)
                .retrieve()
                .body(PolicyDetails.class);
    }

    private PolicyDetails fallbackPolicy(String policyId, Exception ex) {
        return PolicyDetails.unavailable(policyId); // degrade gracefully, don't fail the whole request
    }
}

The three patterns stack deliberately: the bulkhead caps how many concurrent calls to this downstream can be in flight at all (protecting your own service's resources from a single misbehaving dependency); the circuit breaker stops calling a dependency that's clearly failing, giving it time to recover instead of adding load to an already-struggling system; the retry absorbs brief, transient blips. Each solves a different failure mode, and enterprise-grade resilience genuinely needs all three, not just one.

8.8 Saga and Outbox Patterns for Data Consistency

Distributed transactions across service boundaries are a genuine hard problem — there is no two-phase commit across a claims database and a notifications database in a sane microservices architecture. The transactional outbox pattern is the standard, dependable answer: write your state change and the corresponding event to the outbox table in the same local database transaction, then publish the event asynchronously from the outbox, guaranteeing at-least-once delivery without ever risking a state change succeeding while its event silently fails to publish (or vice versa).

ClaimApprovalService.java
@Transactional
public void approve(String claimId, BigDecimal settlementAmount) {
    Claim claim = claimRepository.findById(claimId).orElseThrow();
    claim.approve(settlementAmount);

    // Same local transaction, same database — no distributed transaction required.
    outboxRepository.save(new OutboxEvent(
            UUID.randomUUID().toString(),
            "ClaimApproved",
            claimId,
            toJson(new ClaimApprovedEvent(claimId, settlementAmount, Instant.now())),
            Instant.now()));
}
OutboxPublisher.java — separate poller process
@Scheduled(fixedDelay = 500)
public void publishPendingEvents() {
    List<OutboxEvent> pending = outboxRepository.findUnpublishedOrderByCreatedAt();
    for (OutboxEvent event : pending) {
        streamBridge.send(event.getEventType() + "-out-0", event.getPayload());
        outboxRepository.markPublished(event.getId());
    }
}

For higher-throughput estates, Debezium's Change Data Capture reads the outbox table's write-ahead log directly rather than polling — eliminating the polling latency and load entirely, at the cost of an additional operational component (a Kafka Connect cluster) to run.

ClaimsRagService.java — retrieval and grounded generation
@Service
public class ClaimsRagService {

    private final ChatClient chatClient;
    private final VectorStore vectorStore;

    public ClaimsRagService(ChatClient.Builder builder, VectorStore vectorStore) {
        this.vectorStore = vectorStore;
        this.chatClient = builder.build();
    }

    public String answerCoverageQuestion(String policyId, String question) {
        List<Document> relevant = vectorStore.similaritySearch(SearchRequest.builder()
                .query(question)
                .filterExpression("policyId == '" + policyId + "'")
                .topK(4)
                .build());

        String context = relevant.stream()
                .map(Document::getFormattedContent)
                .collect(Collectors.joining("\n---\n"));

        return chatClient.prompt()
                .system("Answer strictly from the policy wording context provided. If the wording doesn't cover this, say so.")
                .user(u -> u.text("Policy wording:\n{context}\n\nQuestion: {question}")
                        .param("context", context)
                        .param("question", question))
                .call()
                .content();
    }
}

The filterExpression("policyId == '...'") is not a minor detail — it's the multi-tenancy control from Chapter 5 applied to your vector store. Without it, a similarity search could surface and leak another customer's policy wording into a response, which is both a data-protection failure and a correctness failure.

9.6 Guardrails, PII Redaction and GDPR-Safe Prompting

Sending customer personal data to a third-party (or even an internal but separately-hosted) LLM endpoint is a data-processing activity under UK GDPR, and needs the same rigour as any other: a documented legal basis, a Data Processing Agreement with the model provider, and — in most cases — data minimisation applied before the prompt is even constructed.

PiiRedactionService.java
@Service
public class PiiRedactionService {

    private static final Pattern NI_NUMBER = Pattern.compile("[A-CEGHJ-PR-TW-Z]{2}\\d{6}[A-D]");
    private static final Pattern UK_PHONE = Pattern.compile("\\b0\\d{9,10}\\b");

    public String redact(String text) {
        return UK_PHONE.matcher(NI_NUMBER.matcher(text).replaceAll("[NI-NUMBER-REDACTED]"))
                .replaceAll("[PHONE-REDACTED]");
    }
}

Run redaction on both the way in (never send raw National Insurance numbers, full account numbers, or free-text special-category data to a model unless a specific, documented legal basis and DPA covers it) and treat the model's output with equal caution — a model can inadvertently reconstruct or invent plausible-looking personal data in a response, which needs the same review discipline as any other generated content before it reaches a customer.

Common Pitfall "We're just using it internally for case handlers, not customers" is not, by itself, a GDPR exemption — internal processing of personal data by an AI system is still processing, still needs a lawful basis, and still needs to appear in your Record of Processing Activities and Data Protection Impact Assessment. Treat every LLM integration touching real customer data as requiring the same DPIA rigour as any other new system, from day one of the design, not retrofitted before go-live.

9.8 Case Study: An AI-Assisted Claims Triage Service

Pulling this chapter together: a new claim is submitted; the triage service (9.5) scores its risk and recommends a routing action; if risk is elevated, the RAG service (9.3) is consulted for relevant policy-wording context to attach to the case handler's queue item; every model interaction is logged, redacted (9.6), and attributed a cost via observability (9.7); and a circuit breaker (Chapter 8) ensures that if the AI provider has an outage, claims fall back to the standard manual triage queue rather than blocking submission entirely.

@Service
public class ClaimSubmissionOrchestrator {

    private final ClaimTriageService triageService;
    private final ClaimsRagService ragService;
    private final ClaimRepository claimRepository;

    // constructor omitted for brevity

    @CircuitBreaker(name = "aiTriage", fallbackMethod = "manualTriageFallback")
    public Claim submitAndTriage(CreateClaimRequest request) {
        Claim claim = claimRepository.save(Claim.from(request));
        TriageResult triage = triageService.triage(request.description());

        if (triage.riskScore() > 70) {
            String context = ragService.answerCoverageQuestion(request.policyId(), request.description());
            claim.attachHandlerContext(context);
        }

        claim.applyTriage(triage.recommendedAction(), triage.riskScore());
        return claim;
    }

    private Claim manualTriageFallback(CreateClaimRequest request, Exception ex) {
        Claim claim = claimRepository.save(Claim.from(request));
        claim.routeToManualQueue(); // AI unavailable — never block claim submission on it
        return claim;
    }
}

10.1 Containerising Spring Boot 4 with Buildpacks and Dockerfiles

Spring Boot's built-in Cloud Native Buildpacks support remains the lowest-friction path to a production-quality container image, with no Dockerfile to maintain and hand-written layer caching to get wrong:

mvn spring-boot:build-image -Dspring-boot.build-image.imageName=registry.acmebank.co.uk/claims-service:1.4.0

Where a team needs finer control — a specific base image mandated by a security team, or additional OS packages — a hand-written multi-stage Dockerfile remains the alternative, and should follow the same layering principle Buildpacks apply automatically: dependencies in a layer that changes rarely, application code in a layer that changes on every build.

Dockerfile
FROM eclipse-temurin:25-jre-jammy AS base
WORKDIR /app

FROM base AS builder
COPY target/claims-service-*.jar application.jar
RUN java -Djarmode=layertools -jar application.jar extract

FROM base
COPY --from=builder /app/dependencies/ ./
COPY --from=builder /app/spring-boot-loader/ ./
COPY --from=builder /app/snapshot-dependencies/ ./
COPY --from=builder /app/application/ ./
USER 1000:1000
ENTRYPOINT ["java", "org.springframework.boot.loader.launch.JarLauncher"]

layertools extraction splits the JAR into layers by change frequency — dependencies (rarely change), then the application's own classes (change every build) — so a rebuild after a code-only change only pushes a small final layer to your registry, not the whole fat JAR again. Note also USER 1000:1000: running as a non-root user inside the container is a standard requirement in UK enterprise container security policies and Kubernetes Pod Security Standards alike.

deployment.yaml (excerpt)

  name: claims-service

            httpGet: { path: /actuator/health/liveness, port: 8080 }
            initialDelaySeconds: 20
            periodSeconds: 10
          env:
            - name: SPRING_PROFILES_ACTIVE

Spring Boot's Actuator distinguishes readiness (can this pod currently accept traffic — should it be in the load-balancer's rotation) from liveness (is this pod's JVM process healthy at all — should Kubernetes restart it) — always wire these to the correct, distinct probe. A common misconfiguration is pointing both probes at the same generic /actuator/health endpoint, which loses this distinction entirely and can cause Kubernetes to restart a pod that's merely temporarily not ready (e.g. still warming a cache) rather than genuinely dead.

Helm packages this templated, per-environment — a values file per environment (dev/uat/prod) drives replica counts, resource limits, and image tags through the same chart, which is the standard UK enterprise pattern for keeping environment promotion auditable and consistent.

@Service
public class ClaimsService {

    private final Counter claimsSubmittedCounter;
    private final Timer claimProcessingTimer;

    public ClaimsService(MeterRegistry registry) {
        this.claimsSubmittedCounter = Counter.builder("claims.submitted")
                .tag("channel", "api")
                .register(registry);
        this.claimProcessingTimer = Timer.builder("claims.processing.duration")
                .publishPercentileHistogram()
                .register(registry);
    }

    public Claim submit(CreateClaimRequest request) {
        return claimProcessingTimer.record(() -> {
            claimsSubmittedCounter.increment();
            // ... actual submission logic ...
            return doSubmit(request);
        });
    }
}

12.1 Migrating Spring Boot 2 → 3 → 4: A Phased Playbook

Jumping directly from Spring Boot 2 to 4 in one release is rarely the right call for a production system of any real size — too many breaking changes land at once to isolate a regression's cause. The phased approach used across most successful UK enterprise migrations:

  1. Boot 2.7 as the staging point. If you're on an earlier Boot 2.x, upgrade to 2.7 first — the last Boot 2 minor version, and the one Spring's own migration tooling targets as a baseline.
  2. Boot 2.7 → 3.x: the jakarta migration (12.3) happens here, alongside the Java 8/11 → 17 jump (Boot 3's floor). This is almost always the single largest-effort step in the whole sequence — budget accordingly.
  3. Boot 3.x → 4.0: the smaller step. As discussed in Chapter 1, this is comparatively incremental — Virtual Threads, RFC 9457 defaults, and AOT maturity, but no namespace change and minimal API breakage for a codebase already current on Boot 3 patterns.
  4. Java 17 → 21/25 can happen alongside or shortly after the Boot 4 move, since Boot 4 supports the full range — decoupling this from the Boot upgrade itself reduces the size of any single change.

At each step: upgrade on a branch, run the full test suite (Chapter 11's pyramid, especially the Testcontainers-based integration layer, is what actually catches real regressions here), deploy to a UAT environment under representative load, and only then promote — never attempt to also ship new features in the same release as a major framework upgrade.

12.3 javax → jakarta Namespace Migration in Depth

The single most disruptive mechanical change in the Boot 2→3 jump: every javax.persistence.*, javax.validation.*, and javax.servlet.* import needs to become jakarta.persistence.*, jakarta.validation.*, jakarta.servlet.* respectively — a consequence of the Java EE → Jakarta EE transfer to the Eclipse Foundation years earlier finally reaching Spring's own baseline.

Audit every internal shared library dependency before starting this migration; a library maintained by a team that hasn't yet migrated becomes a hard blocker for every consumer, which is why this migration is frequently sequenced organisation-wide as a coordinated programme rather than left to individual teams.

12.5 JVM Tuning for 2026 Hardware and Cloud Pricing

java -XX:+UseZGC -XX:+ZGenerational \
     -Xmx2g -Xms2g \
     -XX:MaxRAMPercentage=75 \
     -jar claims-service.jar

Generational ZGC, now the settled default recommendation for latency-sensitive services on Java 21+, delivers sub-millisecond pause times even on multi-gigabyte heaps — directly relevant to payments and trading paths where a multi-hundred-millisecond G1 pause was historically a real, measurable tail-latency problem. Setting -Xmx/-Xms to the same value avoids heap-resize pauses; MaxRAMPercentage is the right lever in a containerised environment, since it scales relative to the container's memory limit rather than the host's total physical memory, which the JVM cannot see correctly inside a cgroup-constrained container without this flag.

On UK cloud spend specifically: right-sizing heap against actual container memory limits (rather than leaving generous, unused headroom "just in case") directly reduces the memory-driven cost component discussed in Chapter 10.8 — this is one of the few tuning changes that simultaneously improves both latency and cost, rather than trading one for the other.

12.6 Strangler Fig Patterns for Monolith Decomposition

For a large legacy monolith where a big-bang rewrite is both too risky and too slow to get budget approval for, the strangler fig pattern — routing traffic incrementally to new services while the legacy system continues handling everything not yet migrated — remains the dependable, low-risk default across UK modernisation engagements.

  1. Introduce a routing layer (the API gateway from Chapter 8) in front of the monolith, initially proxying 100% of traffic through unchanged.
  2. Identify a genuinely low-risk, well-bounded capability to extract first — not the most valuable, but the one with the clearest boundary and fewest hidden dependencies, to prove the pattern works on your specific estate before tackling anything harder.
  3. Build the new service (following this book's patterns throughout), with the monolith's database as the temporary source of truth if full data migration isn't yet feasible — read-replication or change-data-capture from Chapter 8's outbox discussion bridges this gap.
  4. Route that specific capability's traffic to the new service at the gateway, monitored closely (Chapter 11), with an immediate rollback path to the monolith if anything regresses.
  5. Repeat, capability by capability, until the monolith has been "strangled" down to whatever core genuinely doesn't warrant extraction — some legacy capabilities never will, and that's a legitimate, pragmatic outcome, not a failure of the migration.

12.7 Data Migration Without Downtime

Extending Chapter 5's expand/contract migration pattern to a full strangler-fig data cutover: run the new service against a live-replicated copy of the relevant data (via CDC, as in Chapter 8.8) while dual-writing from the monolith during a transition window, verify the replica's consistency continuously against the source of truth, then cut reads over to the new service's own database, and finally retire the monolith's ownership of that data once confidence is established over a monitored bake-in period — never as a single, irreversible cutover event.

13.1 Case Study: A Tier-1 Bank's Payments Modernisation

Starting point: a 12-year-old Spring Boot 1.5 payments-processing monolith on Java 8, handling several million transactions daily, with a single shared Oracle database and a 90-minute deployment window requiring a full outage.

Approach: the phased migration playbook from Chapter 12, sequenced deliberately conservatively given the regulatory stakes — Boot 1.5 → 2.7 → 3.x (absorbing the jakarta migration and Java 17 jump together) → 4.0, each phase running in shadow (receiving mirrored production traffic, results compared but not acted on) for a full month before being promoted to serve live traffic. The payments-authorisation capability was the first strangler-fig extraction (Chapter 12.6), chosen specifically because it had the clearest bounded context and the fewest hidden dependencies on the rest of the monolith.

Key technical decisions: Virtual Threads over a WebFlux rewrite for the new payments-authorisation service, following the honest trade-off analysis in Chapter 7 — the team judged the hiring and debugging-cost risk of a fully reactive rewrite too high relative to the throughput gain, given Virtual Threads closed most of the practical gap. The transactional outbox pattern (Chapter 8.8) underpinned every state change requiring downstream notification, given the zero-tolerance-for-lost-events requirement in a payments context. Tamper-evident audit logging (Chapter 6.6) was non-negotiable from day one given FCA/PRA obligations.

Outcome pattern: deployment windows moved from a 90-minute full outage to zero-downtime rolling deployments via Kubernetes readiness probes (Chapter 10.3) and canary rollouts (Chapter 10.7); P99 authorisation latency improved measurably once the platform-thread bottleneck was removed by Virtual Threads; and — commercially significant — the migration reduced the specialist-Java-8-and-reactive-skills hiring dependency that had made the legacy system increasingly expensive to staff.

13.2 Case Study: An Insurer's Claims Platform on AKS

Starting point: a greenfield claims platform build for a mid-sized UK general insurer, targeting Azure given the firm's existing Microsoft 365 and Entra ID estate.

Approach: built API-first (Chapter 4.1) from a jointly-owned OpenAPI contract with the firm's broker-portal team, deployed to AKS (Chapter 10.5) with Helm-templated per-environment configuration. Multi-tenancy (Chapter 5.7) via the shared-schema-plus-discriminator pattern, since the platform needed to serve a growing number of intermediary brokers cost-effectively rather than provisioning per-broker infrastructure. Spring AI 2.0 (Chapter 9) was introduced in a second phase for claims triage specifically, wired with the circuit-breaker-protected fallback-to-manual-queue pattern from Chapter 9.8, given the firm's justified caution about AI availability risk on a customer-facing claims path.

Key technical decisions: Azure OpenAI specifically (rather than a third-party provider) for data-residency and existing-enterprise-agreement reasons, with the RAG pipeline (Chapter 9.3) tenant-filtered per broker to prevent any cross-broker policy-wording leakage — treated as a hard security requirement, not an optimisation, given the platform's multi-tenant nature.

Outcome pattern: the platform onboarded new intermediary brokers in days rather than the months typical of the firm's previous per-broker bespoke integrations, and AI-assisted triage measurably reduced average time-to-first-handler-action on straightforward claims, while the manual-fallback design ensured no claim was ever blocked by an AI outage.

13.3 Case Study: A Government Digital Service (GDS-Aligned)

Starting point: a citizen-facing case-management service for a UK government department, procured under standard public-sector frameworks with multiple supplier teams collaborating across the service's lifetime.

Approach: heavy emphasis on the "enterprise-grade" properties from Chapter 1.4 — auditability and GDPR-by-design were treated as first-order requirements from the initial architecture decision records, not retrofitted. Subject Access Request tooling (Chapter 6.5) was built as a first-class feature from the outset, given the statutory one-month SAR response obligation and the sensitivity of the data involved. Contract-first API design (Chapter 4.1) and consumer-driven contract testing (Chapter 8.9) were essential given the multi-supplier delivery model, where different teams built different services against a shared, governance-reviewed contract without a single team having end-to-end visibility.

Key technical decisions: conservative technology choices throughout — Spring MVC with Virtual Threads rather than WebFlux, Maven over Gradle, URI-based API versioning (Chapter 4.5) — deliberately favouring the operational simplicity and broad supplier-team familiarity that Chapter 1.2 identifies as a defining characteristic of public-sector engineering culture, over marginal performance gains from less mainstream choices.

Outcome pattern: the explicit, contract-first governance model allowed genuinely independent supplier teams to deliver against a shared service without the integration breakage typical of loosely-coordinated multi-supplier government programmes, and the SAR tooling built as a feature (rather than a manual process) kept response times reliably within the statutory window even as data volumes grew.

13.4 Case Study: A Retailer's Black-Friday-Scale Checkout

Starting point: a mid-market UK retailer's checkout service, historically over-provisioned year-round to survive a single peak weekend, at significant ongoing cloud cost.

Approach: the checkout-availability path adopted WebFlux deliberately (Chapter 7's genuine-use-case scenario) specifically for its backpressure characteristics under the extreme, spiky concurrency of a flash-sale event — one of the clearer real-world cases where reactive's cost was judged worth paying. Resilience4j bulkheads and circuit breakers (Chapter 8.5) isolated the payment-gateway and stock-reservation dependencies from each other, so a slow payment provider couldn't cascade into stock-reservation failures. Horizontal Pod Autoscaling tied to request-rate and queue-depth signals rather than CPU alone (Chapter 10.8) handled the actual scale-up.

Key technical decisions: native image compilation (Chapter 10.2) specifically for the stock-reservation service, which needed to scale from near-zero to very high replica counts within minutes as peak traffic hit — the cold-start reduction from native image directly translated into faster, cheaper autoscaling response versus the JVM baseline.

Outcome pattern: the platform absorbed peak Black Friday load without the manual, weeks-in-advance over-provisioning the previous architecture required, while reducing average year-round compute cost by right-sizing baseline capacity to genuine steady-state demand rather than peak — directly realising the cost-optimisation guidance from Chapter 10.8.

14.1 Reading UK Job Specs: What "Essential" vs "Desirable" Really Means

UK job specifications, particularly at banks and larger consultancies, are usually written jointly by a hiring manager and an HR/recruitment partner, and the two sections carry genuinely different weight. "Essential" criteria are typically what a CV gets screened against before a human ever reads it properly — missing more than one or two essential items usually means the application doesn't progress, regardless of other strengths. "Desirable" criteria matter far more at interview stage, where they differentiate between two candidates who both clear the essential bar.

UK Job Market Tip Don't self-select out of a role because you're missing one "desirable" item — that section exists to differentiate, not to gatekeep. Do take "essential" seriously; if a spec lists "Spring Boot 3+" as essential and your most recent project used Boot 2, that's worth addressing directly in your application rather than hoping it goes unnoticed, since ATS (applicant tracking system) keyword matching increasingly automates this first filter.

14.2 CV and LinkedIn Positioning for Spring Boot Specialists

14.4 System Design Interviews: Worked Spring Boot Examples

A strong answer, drawing directly on this book's chapters:

  • Reliability: the transactional outbox pattern (Chapter 8.8) to guarantee the claim is durably persisted even if a downstream notification service is down, with Resilience4j bulkheads and circuit breakers (Chapter 8.5) isolating that dependency.
